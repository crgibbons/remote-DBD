    Section Apportionment

    One of the first topics we’ll study is apportionment.
    🔗

    Definition 15. Apportionment.

    The term apportionment refers to the mathematical process of distributing a fixed number of indistinguishable assets among different entities.
    🔗
    Each asset is indistinguishable from any other asset, and no asset can be subdivided.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 16. Is it apportionment?

    For example, suppose Courtney buys 45 boxes of coffee (from the orange and pink doughnut shop down the hill) for the math department to bring to their classes. The department has to come up with a way to decide how many boxes each professor gets, perhaps using the number of students each professor is teaching as the main relevant piece of data to make this decision.
    🔗
    Each professor has to decide how to share the coffee among their classes. This need not be an apportionment problem.
    🔗
    Based on the following table of data, determine how to divvy up the boxes of coffee in different ways. Each box serves 10. Assume every student (and professor) drinks coffee.
    🔗
    Which method or methods seem “fair”? Why?
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Apportionment Terminology

    One of the most famous apportionment problems is the apportionment of seats in the U.S. House of Representatives among the U.S. states. In fact, the terminology for apportionment problems comes from this application.
    🔗

    Definition 18. Apportionment Terms.

    Apportionment Problem
    🔗
    a scenario in which \(m\) identical and \(n\) indivisible items (the “seats”) must be divided among \(n\) entities (the “states”) proportionately (using the “populations”)
    🔗
    States
    🔗
    the entities in the apportionment problem
    🔗
    Seats
    🔗
    the items being apportioned among the states
    🔗
    Apportionment Method
    🔗
    the algorithm determining how to apportion the \(m\) seats to the \(n\) states, guaranteeing exactly \(m\) seats are distributed proporitionaly to each of the \(n\) states based on their populations \(p_s\) for \(1 \le s \le n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Standard Divisor \(D\) and Standard Quota \(Q_s\), Lower Quota\(L_s\), Upper Quota \(U_s\)
    🔗
    \(D = p/m\) where \(p = \sum_{s = 1}^n p_s\) is the total population; \(Q_s = p_s/D\text{,}\) representing the “fair” share of seats for state \(s\) if the seats could be subdivided; \(L_s = \lfloor Q_s \rfloor\text{,}\) the largest integer less than or equal to the standard quota; \(U_s = L_s + 1\text{,}\) the smallest integer strictly greater than the standard quota.
    Aside
    🔗
    Modified Divisor (and friends)
    🔗
    A value \(d\) or \(D'\) other than the standard divisor, used to make the distribution of seats produced by rounding (based on a given convention) add up to \(m\text{.}\) From \(d\) each state gets a modified quota \({Q_s}^\prime\text{,}\) lower quota \({L_s}^\prime\text{,}\) and upper quota \({U_s}^\prime\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Priority
    🔗
    An order by which extra seats are assigned to states. An example is using the fractional part of the quota to determine which states get their upper quota of seats by ranking from largest fracitonal part to smallest.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 19. Revisiting Coffee.

    Taking each professor to be a state and each box of coffee to be a seat, we use the number of students each professor is teaching as the population (plus the professors themselves!). The standard divisor is \(D = \frac{485}{45} = 10 + \frac{35}{485} = 10.\overline{7}\text{.}\)

    Definition 22. Hamilton’s Method.

    Start by giving each state its lower quota. Then, in order of largest fractional part, distribute the remaining seats.

    Three additional methods involve changing the value of \(D\) to come up with a modified divisor \(d\) where some method (rounding conventionally, using the upper quota, or using the lower quota for each state) adds up to the number of seats.     Definition 26. The Geometric Mean.

    The geometric mean of two distinct numbers \(L\) and \(U\) is given by \(c = \sqrt{LU}\text{.}\) If \(q \gt c\text{,}\) round up. If \(q \lt c\text{,}\) round down.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 27. Calculating the Geometric Mean.

    The geometric mean of 3 and 4 is \(\sqrt{3\cdot 4} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.464\text{,}\) while the arithmetic mean is \(\frac{3 + 4}{2} = 3.5\text{.}\) The geometric mean of 5 and 6 is \(\sqrt{5\cdot 6} = \sqrt{30} \approx 5.477\text{,}\) while the arithmetic mean is \(\frac{5+6}{2} = 5.5\text{.}\) The geometric mean of 10 and 11 is \(\sqrt{10\cdot 11} = \sqrt{110} \approx 10.488\text{,}\) while the arithmetic mean is \(\frac{10 + 11}{2} = 10.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 28.

    Definition 29. Huntington–Hill Method (Equal Proportions).

    The Huntington–Hill method is an apportionment method that assigns seats one at a time using priority values based on population and the geometric mean of consecutive seat numbers.
    🔗
    After each state receives one initial seat, additional seats are awarded sequentially to the state with the largest priority value \(\frac{P}{\sqrt{n(n+1)}}\text{,}\) where \(P\) is the state’s population and \(n\) is the number of seats the state currently holds.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 30.

    The Huntington-Hill method is sometimes described using different divisors, which can be confusing at first. This happens because the method has two equivalent formulations.
    🔗
    In the priority formulation, seats are assigned one at a time using the priority value \(\frac{P}{\sqrt{n(n+1)}}\text{,}\) where the divisor \(\sqrt{n(n+1)}\) depends on the state’s current number of seats.
    🔗
    In the rounding formulation, a single global divisor is used to compute quotas, and each quota is rounded up or down depending on whether it lies above or below the geometric mean \(\sqrt{n(n+1)}\text{.}\) Here, the same expression appears as a rounding threshold rather than a priority denominator.
    🔗
    Although the divisors look different in these descriptions, both approaches implement the same rule: rounding is determined by the geometric mean, which balances relative over- and under-representation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Paradoxes

    Quota paradox
    🔗
    This paradox occurs when a state is assigned a number of seats that lies outside its quota—that is, it receives fewer than its lower quota or more than its upper quota. A state’s lower quota is the whole number part (floor) of its standard quota, and its upper quota is the next integer (ceiling). Any apportionment method that violates this rule is said to exhibit the quota paradox. While Hamilton’s method always satisfies the quota rule, several divisor methods can, in certain situations, assign a state a number of seats outside its quota.
    🔗
    Alabama paradox
    🔗
    This paradox occurs when a state gets apportioned fewer seats simply because the total number of seats being apportioned has increased. Under Hamilton’s method, increasing the house size can reshuffle the residues (fractional parts) of the standard quotas; some states can move ahead of others in the priority order for surplus seats, and a state can lose a seat it previously had. A famous historical instance arose in the 1880s when proposed U.S. House sizes led to Alabama receiving fewer seats when the House size increased by one.
    🔗
    Population paradox
    🔗
    This paradox occurs when a state gets apportioned fewer seats simply because its population has increased. More specifically, it describes situations in which state X loses a seat to state Y even though X grew in population at a higher rate than Y. Under Hamilton’s method, changes in populations can change residues and the surplus-seat priority order in a way that produces this outcome.
    🔗
    New-states paradox
    🔗
    This paradox occurs when a state’s apportionment is affected simply by virtue of the fact that a new state (together with its fair share of seats) has been added to the computation. Historically, when Oklahoma joined the Union in 1907, the House size was increased to accommodate Oklahoma’s fair share, but the recalculated apportionment still shifted seats among existing states (for example, Maine gained a seat while New York lost one).
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗

    Subsubsection The Exact Quota

    A central concept in apportionment is the exact quota, denoted \(q_j\text{,}\) which represents the ideal fractional share of units an entity would receive if units were divisible. It is defined by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} q_j = \frac{a_j}{A} M. \end{equation*}
    In an ideal mathematical world, we would have \(m_j = q_j\text{.}\) However, because \(m_j\) must be an integer and \(q_j\) is typically a real number, an apportionment method is required to map the real vector of quotas to an integer vector of allocations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Mathematical Constraints and Properties

    For an apportionment method to be considered fair or valid, it is often expected to satisfy certain mathematical axioms:
    🔗
    • Symmetry. The outcome should not depend on the order in which the entities are listed.
      🔗
    • Homogeneity. If all input values \(a_j\) are multiplied by the same positive constant, the resulting apportionment should remain unchanged.
      🔗
    • The Quota Rule. A method satisfies this rule if each allocation \(m_j\) is either \(\lfloor q_j \rfloor\) or \(\lceil q_j \rceil\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • House Monotonicity. If the total number of units increases from \(M\) to \(M+1\text{,}\) no entity’s allocation \(m_j\) should decrease. This property rules out paradoxes such as the Alabama Paradox.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Error Minimization

    From a mathematical perspective, many apportionment methods can be viewed as attempting to minimize the error between the ideal proportions \(q_j\) and the actual integer allocations \(m_j\text{.}\) This is often modeled using a decomposable error function
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \psi(\mathbf{m}) = \sum_{j=1}^n \varphi_j(m_j), \end{equation*}
    where \(\varphi_j\) measures the severity of the error for entity \(j\text{.}\) For example, the Sainte-Lague method can be characterized as minimizing an error function based on the squared difference between allocated units and exact quotas.

    Subsubsection Formal Mathematical Variables

    🔗

    Subsubsection The (Exact) Quota

    A central concept in apportionment is the exact quota, denoted \(q_s\text{,}\) which represents the ideal fractional share of units an entity would receive if units were divisible. It is defined by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} q_s = \frac{p_s}{P} M. \end{equation*}
    In an ideal mathematical world, we would have \(m_s = q_s\text{.}\) However, because \(m_s\) must be an integer and \(q_s\) is typically a rational number that is not an integer, an apportionment method is required to map the real vector of quotas to an integer vector of allocations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 38. Apportionment Method: a function!

    Fix a positive integer \(M\) and an integer \(n \ge 2\text{.}\) Let \(\mathcal{Q} = \{\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^n : \sum_{s=1}^n q_s = M\}\) denote the space of exact quota vectors, and let \(\mathcal{M} = \{\mathbf{m} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^n : \sum_{s=1}^n m_s = M\}\) denote the set of feasible integer allocations.
    🔗
    An apportionment method is a function
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F : \mathcal{Q} \longrightarrow \mathcal{M} \end{equation*}
    that assigns to each exact quota vector \(\mathbf{q}\) a unique integer allocation \(\mathbf{m} = F(\mathbf{q})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The method \(F\) induces a partition of \(\mathcal{Q}\) into regions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(\mathbf{m}) = \{\mathbf{q} \in Q : F(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{m}\}, \end{equation*}
    called the apportionment polyhedra. Each polyhedron consists of all exact quota vectors that are mapped to the same integer allocation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Apportionment Polyhedra

    An apportionment polyhedron is a geometric region within the space of all possible exact quota vectors \(\mathbf{q}\) that a particular apportionment method maps to a single, fixed integer seat allocation \(\mathbf{m}\text{.}\) In mathematical terms, it consists of all fractional quota vectors that result in the same whole-number distribution of seats.
    🔗

    Subsubsection Geometric Representation

    The concept is best understood by visualizing the space of exact quotas.
    🔗
    • The set \(\mathcal{Q}\text{.}\) This set contains all possible fractional distributions of \(M\) items among \(n\) entities. For example, if there are three parties and \(M = 5\) seats, the set \(\mathcal{Q}\) forms an equilateral triangle in three-dimensional space with vertices at \((5,0,0)\text{,}\) \((0,5,0)\text{,}\) and \((0,0,5)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Integer points. Within this geometric space, the possible integer seat allocations are represented by discrete lattice points corresponding to integer vectors \(\mathbf{m}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Defining the polyhedra. A valid apportionment method partitions the larger region \(Q\) into smaller regions, or polyhedra, each associated with a single integer allocation. Any exact quota vector lying inside a given polyhedron is rounded or mapped to the integer allocation corresponding to that region.
      🔗
    The shape and size of apportionment polyhedra depend on the error function or rounding rule associated with the chosen apportionment method.
    🔗
    • Mapping. A desirable property of an apportionment method is that any exact quota vector that is already an integer vector is mapped to itself.
      🔗
    • Borders. The boundaries between apportionment polyhedra correspond to tie situations, where an exact quota vector may be equally compatible with two or more integer seat allocations. In such cases, the method must include a rule for resolving ties along these boundaries.
      🔗
    To find apportionment polyhedra given an apportionment function, it is useful to introduce barycentric coordinates. Given a value \(M\text{,}\) a barycentric \(n\)-tuple \((q_1,q_2,\cdots,q_m)\) satisfies the property that \(q_i \geq 0\) for each coordinate and \(\sum_{i = 1}^n q_i = M\text{.}\) We can imagine each tuple with an \(M\) in one spot and zeros elsewhere to be sitting at the corner of an \(n\)-dimensional simplex, which results from treating the tuple like the end of a vector with tail at the original in \(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^n\text{.}\) Given a coordinate \(q_i\text{,}\) the face opposite its is the set of all barycentric coordinates with a zero in position \(i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Mathematical Constraints and Properties

    For an apportionment method to be considered fair or valid, it is often expected to satisfy certain mathematical axioms:
    🔗
    • Symmetry. The outcome should not depend on the order in which the entities are listed.
      🔗
    • Homogeneity. If all input values \(q_j\) are multiplied by the same positive constant, the resulting apportionment should remain unchanged.
      🔗
    • The Quota Rule. A method satisfies this rule if each allocation \(m_j\) is either \(\lfloor q_j \rfloor\) or \(\lceil q_j \rceil\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • House Monotonicity. If the total number of units increases from \(M\) to \(M+1\text{,}\) no entity’s allocation \(m_j\) should decrease. This property rules out paradoxes such as the Alabama Paradox.
      🔗
    The Balinski-Young Theorem essentially says that there is no apportionment rule that satisfies all of these criteria all the time!
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Hamilton’s Method as an Apportionment Function

    Fix \(M\) seats and \(n\) entities. Given an exact quota vector \(\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{Q}\text{,}\) Hamilton’s method proceeds as follows:
    🔗
    1. Assign each entity its lower quota \(m_j = \lfloor q_j \rfloor\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Distribute the remaining seats, one at a time, to the entities with the largest fractional remainders \(q_j - \lfloor q_j \rfloor\text{.}\)
      🔗
    This defines an apportionment method \(F_{\text{Ham}} : \mathcal{Q} \to \mathcal{M}\text{,}\) since every quota vector is mapped to a unique integer allocation.
    🔗
    Geometrically, the apportionment polyhedra for Hamilton’s method are regions determined by inequalities comparing fractional parts of the quotas. The boundaries between polyhedra occur where two or more remainders are equal, leading to ties in the final seat assignment.
    🔗
    Because these boundaries depend on global comparisons of remainders, the resulting polyhedra need not arise from minimizing a single global error function. This explains why Hamilton’s method can satisfy the Quota Rule while still exhibiting paradoxes such as the Alabama Paradox.
    🔗
    Example 41. The Hamilton Polyhedron for an example..
    Suppose we have \(M=15\) boxes of coffee to distribute among three people (Ahmed, Gibbons, and Tripp) in proportion to their “need scores” \(\mathbf{a}=(35,55,58)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The total is \(A = 35+55+58 = 148\text{,}\) so the exact quotas are \(q_j = \dfrac{a_j}{A}M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using Hamilton’s (largest remainder) method, we first assign lower quotas: \((3,5,5)\text{,}\) which totals \(13\) boxes, leaving \(2\) boxes to distribute. The two largest remainders are Tripp’s and Gibbons’s, so each receives one additional box.
    🔗
    The final allocation is
    \begin{equation*} (m_{\text{Ahmed}},m_{\text{Gibbons}},m_{\text{Tripp}}) = (3,6,6). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consider Hamilton’s method on the quota simplex \(\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_A,q_G,q_T)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^3:\ q_A+q_G+q_T=15\}\text{.}\) We describe the region of quota vectors that Hamilton maps to the allocation \((3,6,6)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For Hamilton’s method to output \((3,6,6)\text{,}\) the lower-quota step must first produce
    • Two seats left: \((2,5,6)\text{,}\) \((2,6,5)\text{,}\) or \((3,5,5)\text{;}\)
      🔗
    • One seat left: \((2,6,6)\text{,}\) \((3,5,6)\text{,}\) or \((3,6,5)\text{;}\)
      🔗
    • No seats left: \((3,6,6)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    In our example, we have the scenario that the lower quotas produce \((3,5,5)\text{,}\) and the remainders satisfy \(r_G \gt r_A\text{,}\) \(r_T > r_A\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3 \le q_A < 4,\qquad 5 \le q_G < 6,\qquad 5 \le q_T < 6, \qquad q_A+q_G+q_T=15. \end{equation*}
    Write the remainders as \(r_A=q_A-3\text{,}\) \(r_G=q_G-5\text{,}\) and \(r_T=q_T-5\text{.}\) To award the two remaining seats to Gibbons and Tripp (and not to Ahmed), we need the two inequalities
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r_G > r_A,\qquad r_T > r_A. \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, these are linear conditions on the quotas:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} q_G - q_A > 2,\qquad q_T - q_A > 2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, part of the (open) Hamilton region mapping to \((3,6,6)\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(3,6,6) \supseteq \left\{ (q_A,q_G,q_T)\in Q: \begin{array}{l} 3 \le q_A < 4,\ 5 \le q_G < 6,\ 5 \le q_T < 6,\\ q_G-q_A > 2,\ q_T-q_A > 2 \end{array} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    The boundary of this region consists of tie situations, such as \(r_G=r_A\) or \(r_T=r_A\text{,}\) together with the grid oundaries where a quota crosses an integer and a lower quota changes (for example, \(q_G=6\)). A complete closed polyhedron requires a specified tie-breaking rule on these boundary sets.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Using Apportionment Polyhedra to Assess Fairness

    The apportionment polyhedron provides a geometric framework for assessing the fairness of an apportionment method by describing how the method rounds exact quotas—the ideal fractional shares of seats—into whole-number allocations. By analyzing the shapes and boundaries of these polyhedra, mathematicians and social scientists can determine which methods minimize rounding error and which are susceptible to representational paradoxes.
    🔗

    Subsubsection Visualizing Rounding and Error

    Geometrically, the space of all possible exact quota vectors \(Q\) is partitioned into regions, or polyhedra, each associated with a single integer seat allocation.
    🔗
    • Mapping fairness. A well-behaved apportionment method maps any exact quota vector lying inside a given polyhedron to the integer allocation at its center. Fairness can then be assessed by measuring the gravity of the error, defined as the distance or discrepancy between the ideal quota \(q_j\) and the resulting integer allocation \(m_j\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Defining boundaries. The boundaries between polyhedra correspond to tie situations, in which an exact quota vector may be equally compatible with more than one integer allocation. A fair method must resolve such ties using a consistent rule, rather than an arbitrary choice.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Comparing Methodological Justness

    Different apportionment methods correspond to different error functions, and these choices lead to distinct polyhedral decompositions of the quota space. This geometric perspective allows for direct mathematical comparison between methods.
    🔗
    • Sainte-Lague versus Hare. Different methods induce different polyhedral grids. The Sainte‑Laguë method is designed to minimize relative error and, in three-entity settings, produces a hexagonal tiling that is often regarded as neutral with respect to entity size. In contrast, rounding methods such as Hare’s method focus on greatest remainders and are frequently analyzed using \(\ell_p\)-norms—such as Euclidean distance—to identify the integer point closest to a given exact quota.
      🔗
    • Bias detection. Geometric analysis can reveal systematic biases in an apportionment method. For example, linear divisor methods may favor smaller entities or larger entities depending on the placement of their polyhedral boundaries, often parametrized by an initial divisor offset.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Identifying Paradoxes

    Apportionment polyhedra are also a useful tool for determining whether a method satisfies common axioms of fairness.
    🔗
    • The Quota Rule. Polyhedral analysis can be used to check whether allocations always lie between the floor and ceiling of the exact quotas.
      🔗
    • House monotonicity. Examining how polyhedral boundaries change as the total number of seats \(M\) increases helps determine whether a method avoids decreases in allocation when additional seats are added.
      🔗
    • The Alabama Paradox. Poorly aligned polyhedral boundaries can lead to paradoxical outcomes in which increasing the total number of seats causes an entity to lose a seat, a phenomenon observed in historical legislative apportionments.
      🔗
    A contemporary illustration of these issues arose in the 2020 Iowa Democratic caucuses. The use of Hamilton’s method, which satisfies the Quota Rule but is geometrically vulnerable to paradoxes, produced several aggregation anomalies in which the sum of local allocations failed to align with statewide totals.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Affine Sets, Simplices, and Barycentric Coordinates

    These notes assume familiarity with vectors, matrices, linear independence, and basic multivariable geometry.
    🔗
    In linear algebra, a subspace must contain the origin and be closed under linear combinations. Many geometric objects we care about are shifted versions of subspaces.
    🔗
    This leads to the idea of an affine set.
    🔗

    Definition 43. Affine Combination.

    A vector of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1 v_1 + \lambda_2 v_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k v_k \end{equation*}
    is called an affine combination of \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_1+\lambda_2+\cdots+\lambda_k = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 44. Affine Hull.

    The affine hull of points \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) is the set
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{aff}(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i v_i : \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = 1 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 45.

    Two distinct points determine a line, three noncollinear points determine a plane, and four noncoplanar points determine a three-dimensional affine space.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 46. Affine Independence.

    Points \(v_0,\dots,v_n\) are affinely independent if the vectors
    \begin{equation*} v_1-v_0,\; v_2-v_0,\; \dots,\; v_n-v_0 \end{equation*}
    are linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 47. Convex Set.

    A set \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) is convex if for any \(x,y \in C\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (1-t)x+ty \in C \quad\text{for all }0\le t\le 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 48. Convex Combination.

    A convex combination is an affine combination where all coefficients are nonnegative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_i \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A simplex is the higher-dimensional analogue of a triangle or tetrahedron.
    🔗

    Definition 49. Simplex.

    If \(v_0,\dots,v_n\) are affinely independent points in \(\mathbb{R}^m\text{,}\) then the simplex they determine is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{conv}(v_0,\dots,v_n) = \left\{ \sum_{i=0}^n \lambda_i v_i : \lambda_i \ge 0, \; \sum_{i=0}^n \lambda_i = 1 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 50.

    1. A line segment is a \(1\)-simplex.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. A triangle is a \(2\)-simplex.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A tetrahedron is a \(3\)-simplex.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 51. Standard Simplex.

    The standard \(n\)-simplex is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta_n = \left\{ (x_0,\dots,x_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1} : x_i\ge0, \; \sum_{i=0}^n x_i = 1 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Barycentric coordinates express a point relative to the vertices of a simplex.
    🔗

    Definition 52. Barycentric Coordinates.

    Suppose \(v_0,\dots,v_n\) are affinely independent. Every point \(p\) in the affine hull can be written uniquely as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p = \lambda_0 v_0 + \lambda_1 v_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n v_n \end{equation*}
    with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_0+\cdots+\lambda_n=1. \end{equation*}
    The numbers \(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_n\) are called the barycentric coordinates of \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If all barycentric coordinates are positive, then the point lies in the interior of the simplex. If some coordinate is zero, then the point lies on a face of the simplex.
    🔗

    Example 53.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} v_0=(0,0), \qquad v_1=(1,0), \qquad v_2=(0,1). \end{equation*}
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p=(0.2,0.3). \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p = 0.5v_0 + 0.2v_1 + 0.3v_2. \end{equation*}
    Since the coefficients are nonnegative and sum to \(1\text{,}\) the point lies inside the triangle.
    🔗
    🔗
    To compute barycentric coordinates, solve the system
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p = \sum_{i=0}^n \lambda_i v_i \end{equation*}
    together with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda_0+\cdots+\lambda_n=1. \end{equation*}
    A convenient matrix form is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} & & \\ v_0 & v_1 & \cdots v_n \\ & & \\ 1 & 1 & \cdots 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_0\\ \lambda_1\\ \vdots\\ \lambda_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Affine independence guarantees a unique solution.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Barycentric-Compatible Functions

    Barycentric coordinates are especially useful because they allow us to interpolate between objects. In many applications, we have two functions that assign points in a simplex, and we would like to move continuously between them.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f,g : X \to \mathbb{R}^n \end{equation*}
    are functions whose outputs lie in a convex set, such as a simplex. For any parameter \(0 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) define a new function
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} h_t(x) = (1-t)f(x) + tg(x). \end{equation*}
    Since the coefficients \(1-t\) and \(t\) are nonnegative and sum to \(1\text{,}\) the point \(h_t(x)\) is a convex combination of \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Geometrically, for each fixed \(x\text{,}\) the point \(h_t(x)\) travels along the line segment joining \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 54. Barycentric-Compatible Functions.

    Two functions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f,g : X \to \Delta_n \end{equation*}
    are called barycentrically compatible if for every \(x \in X\) and every \(0 \le t \le 1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (1-t)f(x) + tg(x) \end{equation*}
    also lies in \(\Delta_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Equivalently, the line segment joining \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) remains entirely inside the simplex.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 55.

    Because simplices are convex sets, any two functions whose values lie in the same simplex are automatically barycentrically compatible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 56. Probability Distribution Example.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta_2 = \left\{ (x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3 : x+y+z=1,\; x,y,z \ge 0 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    A point in \(\Delta_2\) can be interpreted as a probability distribution on three outcomes.
    🔗
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = (0.7,0.2,0.1), \qquad g(x) = (0.1,0.3,0.6). \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} h_{1/2}(x) = \frac12 f(x) + \frac12 g(x) = (0.4,0.25,0.35). \end{equation*}
    This point is still in the simplex because its coordinates remain nonnegative and sum to \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 57. Paths Inside a Simplex.

    Let \(f,g : [0,1]\to\Delta_2\text{.}\) For each input value \(x\text{,}\) the interpolation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} h_t(x) = (1-t)f(x)+tg(x) \end{equation*}
    determines a path inside the simplex connecting the two images.
    🔗
    As \(t\) varies from \(0\) to \(1\text{,}\) the point moves continuously from \(f(x)\) to \(g(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A Practical Guide to Solving Systems of Linear Functionals

    Suppose we have several linear functionals
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_1,\varphi_2,\dots,\varphi_m : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}. \end{equation*}
    A system of linear functionals asks us to find all vectors satisfying equations or inequalities involving these maps.
    🔗
    Geometrically, such systems describe intersections of hyperplanes and half-spaces.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Writing Functionals as Dot Products

    Every linear functional on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) can be written as a dot product
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi(x)=a\cdot x \end{equation*}
    for some vector \(a\in\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 61.

    The functional
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi(x,y,z)=2x-y+3z \end{equation*}
    corresponds to the coefficient vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (2,-1,3). \end{equation*}
    In matrix form,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ z \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 1: Convert to Matrix Form
    🔗
    Suppose we want to solve
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_1(x,y,z)=1, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_2(x,y,z)=4, \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_1(x,y,z)=x+y+z, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_2(x,y,z)=2x-y+z. \end{equation*}
    Place the coefficient vectors into a matrix:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1\\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Then solve the matrix equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}= \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ z \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b}= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Step 2: Row Reduce
    🔗
    Form the augmented matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\ 2 & -1 & 1 & 4 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    Perform the row operation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1. \end{equation*}
    This gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & -3 & -1 & 2 \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}
    The second equation becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -3y-z=2. \end{equation*}
    Let \(z=t\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=-\frac{2+t}{3}. \end{equation*}
    Substitute into the first equation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+y+z=1. \end{equation*}
    Solving for \(x\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\frac{5-2t}{3}. \end{equation*}
    The solution set is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left( \frac{5-2t}{3}, -\frac{2+t}{3}, t \right). \end{equation*}
    Geometric Interpretation
    🔗
    Each equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi(x)=b \end{equation*}
    defines a hyperplane.
    🔗
    • One equation in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) defines a plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Two independent equations typically intersect in a line.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Three independent equations typically intersect in a point.
      🔗
      🔗
    Inequalities and Half-Spaces
    🔗
    Conditions such as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi(x)\ge c \end{equation*}
    describe half-spaces.
    🔗
    Example 62.
    The inequality
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+y+z \le 1 \end{equation*}
    cuts space into two regions separated by the plane
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+y+z=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Systems of inequalities therefore describe convex regions.
    🔗
    Systems Inside a Simplex
    🔗
    Suppose we work in the standard simplex
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta_2 = \left\{ (x,y,z): x+y+z=1, \; x,y,z\ge0 \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Now add the inequalities
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x\ge1, \qquad 4y<2. \end{equation*}
    Dividing by \(4\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\ge\frac14, \qquad y<\frac12. \end{equation*}
    These inequalities carve out a smaller region inside the simplex.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Strategy for Allocation Problems

    In allocation problems, systems often involve:
    🔗
    1. The simplex equation
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} x_1+\cdots+x_n=1. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    2. Nonnegativity conditions
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} x_i\ge0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. Floor constraints
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} m_i \le Mx_i < m_i+1. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    4. Remainder comparisons
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} Mx_i-\lfloor Mx_i\rfloor \ge Mx_j-\lfloor Mx_j\rfloor. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Practical Workflow

    Subsubsection Step A: Solve the Affine Constraints

    Usually begin with the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1+\cdots+x_n=1. \end{equation*}
    This reduces the dimension by one.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Step B: Add Interval Constraints

    Suppose an allocation requires
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} m_i \le Mx_i < m_i+1. \end{equation*}
    Dividing by \(M\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{m_i}{M} \le x_i < \frac{m_i+1}{M}. \end{equation*}
    These inequalities define slabs bounded by parallel hyperplanes.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Step C: Add Remainder Comparisons

    Compare the fractional parts of the quota coordinates.
    🔗
    Example 63.
    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lfloor4x\rfloor=1, \qquad \lfloor4y\rfloor=0. \end{equation*}
    Then the inequality
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x-\lfloor4x\rfloor \ge 4y-\lfloor4y\rfloor \end{equation*}
    becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4x-1 \ge 4y. \end{equation*}
    Rearranging gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x-y\ge\frac14. \end{equation*}
    Inside a fixed slab region, the floor functions become constants, so the boundaries become linear.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Systems of linear functionals partition space into regions bounded by hyperplanes.
    🔗
    Inside each region:
    🔗
    • the inequalities are consistent,
      🔗
      🔗
    • the allocation rule is constant,
      🔗
      🔗
    • and the geometry is convex.
      🔗
      🔗
    This viewpoint turns allocation methods into geometric partitions of simplices.
    🔗
