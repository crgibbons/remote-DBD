    Subsection Large Language Models and DimensionalityAI Ethics and the Global Data Supply Chain: A Consolidated Guide

    The training corpora for modern language models are huge. For example, the training corpus for GPT-3 contains 300 billion tokens. The rule of thumb for tokens-to-words conversion is that 1 token corresponds to about 0.75 words, so the training corpus for GPT-3 contains about 225 billion words. If this were a book, Wolfram Alpha estimates that it would be about 308 million pages, which would talk about 1600 years of silent reading (2900 years in audio book format, and 7100 years if you were to try to type it out).
    🔗
    BERT, from 2018, was trained on a corpus of 3.3 billion words from the English Wikipedia, which is about 0.0015\% of the size of the GPT-3 training corpus. Larger models rely on illegally scraped data, which is not publicly available, so we don’t know the exact size of their training corpora. But we do know that large libraries, like Library of Congress, contain about 17 million books, which is about 10 billion words, so the training corpora for large language models are at least an order of magnitude larger than the entire Library of Congress.
    🔗
    The training of large language models is also very expensive. For example, the training of GPT-3 is estimated to have cost about 4.6 million dollars, and the training of GPT-4 is estimated to have cost about 100 million dollars. The training of large language models also requires a lot of computational resources, which are not accessible to most researchers and practitioners. For example, the training of GPT-3 is estimated to have required about 355 years of GPU time, and the training of GPT-4 is estimated to have required about 10,000 years of GPU time.
    🔗
    The human cost of training large language models is also very high, as it requires a lot of human labor to collect and preprocess the training data, and to fine-tune the models for specific tasks. For example, the training of GPT-3 is estimated to have required about 10,000 hours of human labor, and the training of GPT-4 is estimated to have required about 100,000 hours of human labor. This labor includes tasks such as data collection, data cleaning, data annotation, and model evaluation, which are all essential for the successful training of large language models. Filtering for graphic or inappropriate content is also a significant part of the human labor involved in training large language models, as it is important to ensure that the training data does not contain harmful or offensive content that could be learned by the model and reproduced in its outputs.
    🔗
    This section explores the ethical implications of artificial intelligence, ranging from college campus algorithms to the global labor force. Discussion questions and insights based on the Pulitzer Center investigation into the “Platform Proletariat” in Brazil.
    🔗
    The Army of Workers.
    🔗
    The “magic” of AI is an illusion. It does not appear “magically” through machines; it requires an army of workers to comb through prodigious amounts of data to teach systems what is acceptable.
    Modern Assembly Line.
    🔗
    Like the Industrial Revolution, workers often perform repetitive “microtasks” (tightening a bolt) without knowing if they are training systems for surveillance and repression.
    Labor Rights and Inequality in the Global South.
    🔗
    The AI data supply chain relies on underpaid workers in countries like Brazil rather than Silicon Valley offices.
    Precarious Work.
    🔗
    Workers are paid pennies for complex tasks and face issues like non-payment and strict non-disclosure agreements that prevent them from speaking out about their working conditions.
    Addictive Platforms.
    🔗
    Some platforms embed microtasks that addict children and teens who work for hours for very little pay.
    Quality vs. Accuracy.
    🔗
    Poor working conditions directly impact the quality of AI outputs.
    The Opaque Chain.
    🔗
    The chain of command is intentionally secretive, involving tech giants, intermediary platforms (like Appen), and isolated workers

    Subsection Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and \(t\)-Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (\(t\)-SNE)

    Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and \(t\)-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (\(t\)-SNE) are both methods for reducing the dimension of a dataset. However, they preserve different kinds of structure.
    🔗
    In this worksheet, we compute PCA exactly for a small dataset and then approximate the behavior of t-SNE using neighbor probabilities.
    🔗
    PCA identifies directions of maximum variation in a dataset. It finds the directions in which the data spreads out the most and projects the data onto those directions to reduce its dimension. The linear algebra required to make this happen? Eigenstuff! This allows us to take linear combinations of the “features” (the measured properties) to create new variables, where fewer variables are needed to explain most of the behavior.
    🔗

    Definition 51.

    When working with data, we are often interested in measures of central tendency and measures of spread. Informally, these measures help us answer the questions, “Where is the middle of the data?” and “How spread out is the data?” Let \(X\) be data matrix where each column corresponds to a feature (or variable) and each row corresponds to an observation (or value). We refer to a column of \(X\) as a feature vector, denoted \(\bar{x}\text{.}\) Some measures of central tendency include
    1. The mean for a feature \(x\text{:}\) \(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The median for a feature \(x\text{:}\) the value that splits the data into two equal halves.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The mode for a feature \(x\text{:}\) the most frequently occurring value.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. The midrange for a feature \(x\text{:}\) the average of the minimum and maximum values.
      🔗
      🔗
    Some measures of spread include
    1. The variance for a feature \(x\text{:}\) \(\operatorname{Var}(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The standard deviation for a feature \(x\text{:}\) \(\operatorname{SD}(x) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The covariance of two features \(x\) and \(y\text{:}\) \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    These measures of central tendency and spread are important for understanding the structure of a dataset and for performing dimensionality reduction techniques like PCA. Sometimes we do some preprocessing to make the data easier to work with. Two common transformations are centering and standardizing a feature. These transformations can be expressed in terms of the mean and standard deviation of a feature.
    1. Centering a feature: replacing \(x_i\) with \(x_i - \bar{x}\) for each \(x_i \in x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Standardizing a feature: replacing \(x_i\) with \(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\operatorname{SD}(x)}\) for each \(x_i \in x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Checkpoint 55.

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.
    🔗
    The eigenspaces of the covariance matrix correspond to directions in which the data varies. The eigenvalues give the amount of variation in the direction(s) of the corresponding eigenvector(s). Recall that an eigenspace \(E_\lambda = \{ v \, | \, Av = \lambda v\}\text{.}\) For working with data, we often choose a basis of linearly independent normalized eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue \(\lambda\text{.}\) (Normalized means that the eigenvectors satisfy \(\sum_{i = 1}^n v_i^2 = 1\text{.}\)) Even though zero is never an eigenvector, we include it in the eigenspace to ensure the eigenspace is a vector space.
    🔗
    In this case, each eigenspace is one-dimensional, meaning that each eigenspace has a single eigenvector (up to scaling) that forms a basis for the eigenspace.

    \(t\)-SNE.
    🔗
    Using \(t\)-SNE embeddings, we can visualize high-dimensional data in two or three dimensions while preserving local structure. The key idea is to convert distances between points in the original high-dimensional space into probabilities that represent similarities between points. Then, \(t\)-SNE finds a low-dimensional embedding that preserves these similarities as closely as possible.
    🔗
    Informally, a \(t\)-distribution is a probability distribution that has heavier tails than a normal distribution. This means that it assigns more probability to points that are far away from the mean compared to a normal distribution, and that’s useful when we don’t know the true distribution of the data and want to be more robust to outliers.
    🔗
    If you have taken statistics, you may have encountered Student’s \(t\) test, which is a statistical test used to determine if there is a significant difference between the means of two groups. The \(t\)-distribution is named for the work of William Sealy Gosset, who published under the pseudonym “Student” to avoid conflicts with his employer, Guinness Brewery (he held the title of Head Brewer; look what you can do with a math-stats background!) For more fun statistical history, check out the book How Data Happened by Chris Wiggins and Matthew L. Jones. Why was Guinness employing statisticians? They wanted to improve the quality of their beer, and the challenge faced by statisticians employeed by the brewery was that they had very small sample sizes to work with, which made it difficult to draw reliable conclusions about the quality of the beer. Unlike governments at the time, which used census data (and computing, such as it was!) to gather information about the population, Guinness wanted to save money by using small samples to make decisions about their beer production.
